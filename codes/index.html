01Pulseless VF / VT

CALL A CODE · START CPR · ATTACH THE DEFIBRILLATOR
NAME
"This looks like VF. Start compressions and get ready to defib. I'll get us organized and I need your help." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Compressor · airway · meds/IV-IO · defibrillator/recorder — assign aloud, rotate compressor every 2 min.
AIM
The next priority during compressions is defib.

0:00

NEXT SHOCK: 120 J (ZOLL, shock #1)
AT THE RHYTHM CHECK — WHAT DO YOU SEE?
DEFIBRILLATION
  • ZOLL Rectilinear Biphasic (LRH's device) — 1st shock 120 J → 2nd shock 150 J → 3rd and all subsequent shocks 200 J.
  • Different manufacturer/device on your unit? Use its manufacturer label instead — energies are device-specific.
  • Resume compressions immediately after each shock — minimize the pause to <10 s.
EPINEPHRINE
  • 1 mg IV/IO (1:10,000, i.e. 0.1 mg/mL — 10 mL prefilled syringe), every 3–5 min, flush with 20 mL.
  • Typically first given after the 2nd shock, without interrupting compressions.
ANTIARRHYTHMIC
  • Amiodarone — 1st dose 300 mg IV/IO push after the 3rd shock; 2nd dose 150 mg after a later shock if still in VF/VT. Cumulative max 2.2 g/24 h.
  • Lidocaine (alternative) — 1st dose 1–1.5 mg/kg; 2nd dose 0.5–0.75 mg/kg. Max 3 mg/kg total.
HIGH-QUALITY CPR TARGETS
  • Rate 100–120/min · depth 5–6 cm (2–2.4 in) · full recoil · minimize interruptions.
  • Rotate the compressor every 2 min cycle to prevent fatigue-related decay in compression quality.
REVERSIBLE CAUSES — H's AND T'sCHECK EVERY CYCLE
Sources: 2020 American Heart Association Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care — Adult Cardiac Arrest Algorithm (VF/pVT pathway): epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 min, amiodarone 300 mg then 150 mg, lidocaine 1–1.5 mg/kg then 0.5–0.75 mg/kg (max 3 mg/kg), CPR rate 100–120/min and depth 5–6 cm, and the H's-and-T's reversible-cause list. Defibrillation energies (120 J → 150 J → 200 J) per ZOLL's published Rectilinear Biphasic ACLS defibrillation protocol for LRH's ZOLL devices. Energy levels are device-specific — verify against your unit's manufacturer label and LRH's own ACLS order set.

02PEA / Asystole

NON-SHOCKABLE · NO SHOCK · NO AMIODARONE
NAME
"No pulse, non-shockable rhythm. Continuing CPR — no shock." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Compressor · airway · meds/IV-IO · recorder — assign aloud, rotate compressor every 2 min.
AIM
No shock. Find and fix a reversible cause. Epinephrine and high-quality CPR are the priorities.
0:00
CPR CYCLE — CHECK RHYTHM/PULSE AT 2:00
AT THE RHYTHM CHECK — WHAT DO YOU SEE?
ALGORITHM — NON-SHOCKABLE ARREST
  • Continue high-quality CPR; attach monitor. Rhythm is PEA (organized activity, no pulse) or asystole (flatline) — no defibrillation, no amiodarone/lidocaine for either.
  • Get IV/IO access; give epinephrine as soon as available — do not delay compressions to get it.
  • Consider an advanced airway + waveform capnography. If PETCO₂ stays persistently <10 mmHg, that's a cue to improve CPR quality, not a reason to stop.
  • Every ~2 min: check rhythm. For PEA, check pulse at the same time as the rhythm check (an organized rhythm may be perfusing). For asystole, only check pulse if an organized rhythm appears.
  • Resume CPR immediately after each check; repeat epinephrine every 3–5 min; keep actively searching for and treating a reversible cause throughout — don't save the H's/T's for "later."
  • If the rhythm becomes shockable (VF/pVT) at any check, cross over to the VF/VT card and defibrillate.
REVERSIBLE CAUSES — H's AND T'sFIND IT OR YOU WON'T GET ROSC
Sources: 2020 AHA Guidelines for CPR and ECC — Adult Cardiac Arrest Algorithm, non-shockable (PEA/asystole) branch: no defibrillation/amiodarone/lidocaine, epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 min, simultaneous rhythm+pulse check for PEA, high-quality CPR, H's-and-T's reversible-cause search (ACLS Certification Assoc. summary; ACLS-Algorithms.com, PEA).

03Symptomatic Bradycardia

NAME
"Symptomatic bradycardia — getting atropine and the pacer pads on." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Monitor/pacer pads · IV access · meds · 12-lead — assign aloud.
AIM
Treat only if the bradycardia is causing signs/symptoms. Don't let atropine delay pacing in high-grade block.
ATROPINE 1 mg IV — REPEAT q3–5 MIN
MAX TOTAL 3 mg
GIVEN
0 mg
MAX 3 mg REACHED — atropine won't help further. Move to pacing / dopamine / epinephrine now.
⚠ Atropine is usually ineffective for Mobitz II 2nd-degree block or 3rd-degree (complete) block with a wide QRS/new bundle-branch block. Do not delay pacing for these rhythms — go straight to transcutaneous pacing.
IF ATROPINE INEFFECTIVE / CONTRAINDICATED
  • Transcutaneous pacing — pads on, start ~60–80/min, titrate to capture (electrical + mechanical/pulse). Sedate/analgese if patient is awake — pacing is painful.
  • Dopamine infusion 5–20 mcg/kg/min IV, titrate to response.
  • Epinephrine infusion 2–10 mcg/min IV, titrate to response.
  • Get expert consultation early; prepare for transvenous pacing if a temporary transcutaneous bridge won't be enough.
SEARCH FOR A CAUSE — "BRADI"
Sources: 2020 AHA Adult Bradycardia Algorithm — atropine 1 mg IV q3–5min (max 3 mg; increased from the older 0.5 mg dose in the 2015/2020 update), dopamine 5–20 mcg/kg/min, epinephrine 2–10 mcg/min, and the Mobitz-II/3rd-degree pacing caveat (AHA Bradycardia Algorithm PDF). "BRADI" differential mnemonic and toxicology antidote doses are supplementary EM teaching, not part of the AHA algorithm itself (EM Cases; WikEM) — verify antidote doses with pharmacy before administering.

04Suspected Acute Stroke

CODE STROKE · TIME IS BRAIN
NAME
"Code stroke — I need the exact last-known-well time and a stat CT." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Finger-stick glucose · IV access/labs · CT coordination · recorder (last-known-well time) — assign aloud.
AIM
Rule out hypoglycemia, get the CT, nail down the last-known-well time — it decides everything downstream.

IMMEDIATE SEQUENCE
  • Finger-stick glucose immediately — hypoglycemia is the classic stroke mimic; treat and reassess before proceeding.
  • FAST/BE-FAST exam, NIHSS if trained. STAT non-contrast CT head — don't wait on labs to move to CT.
  • Labs in parallel: coags/INR, platelets, renal function (don't delay CT/lytic decision waiting on these unless on anticoagulation or renal disease known).
  • NIHSS ≥6 or FAST-ED ≥4, with TLKW <24 h → add CTA head/neck to the non-contrast CT order (LVO screen for thrombectomy).
  • BP goal before lytic: <185/110 mmHg. Labetalol 10–20 mg IV over 1–2 min — may repeat ×1, then infusion 2–8 mg/min if still needed — or nicardipine infusion 5 mg/hr, titrate by 2.5 mg/hr q5–15min to a max of 15 mg/hr. Nitroprusside, hydralazine, or enalaprilat are third-line options. After lytic: keep <180/105 mmHg for 24 h.
  • No hemorrhage on CT + within window + no exclusions → LRH's stroke protocol uses tenecteplase (TNK) as the lytic agent: 0.25 mg/kg IV, single bolus, max 25 mg. Review the full inclusion/exclusion checklist on the Stroke Box before giving it, and document the TNK decision and consent. (Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg IV, max 90 mg — 10% bolus over 1 min, remainder over 60 min — remains an accepted alternative at centers not yet converted to TNK.)
  • Thrombectomy candidates — NIHSS ≥6 or FAST-ED ≥4, LVO on CTA/MRA, TLKW <24 h — and any patient who received TNK: arrange transfer to a tertiary center. Ask the HUC to page TeleNeurology and push images. Dartmouth Transfer Center: (877) 999-9870.
  • Admit to a monitored/stroke unit bed if not transferring; neuro checks per protocol.
KEY LYTIC EXCLUSIONS (not exhaustive — confirm full checklist)
COMPLICATIONS — NEUROLOGIC DECLINE AFTER TNK
  • Any neurologic decline post-TNK → STAT repeat head CT and send ICH labs immediately.
  • Symptomatic ICH: labs — CBC, PT, PTT, fibrinogen, type & screen. Cryoprecipitate 10 units IV over 10–30 min, repeat to a fibrinogen goal of 150–200 mg/dL. TXA 10–15 mg/kg IV over 10–20 min. Manage BP, transfuse as needed, and call neurosurgery.
  • TNK-associated orolingual angioedema: Anaphylaxis box to bedside. Consider intubation early if edema is progressing rapidly (within 30 min) and involves the larynx, palate, floor of mouth, or oropharynx — don't wait for severe stridor. Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV, diphenhydramine 50 mg IV, famotidine 20 mg IV. Consider epinephrine 0.1% 0.3 mL SC and/or racemic epinephrine 0.5 mL by nebulizer.
Sources: Littleton Regional Hospital Stroke Alert Role-Based Checklist (MD/PA/APRN) — TNK 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus (max 25 mg) as LRH's primary lytic agent, NIHSS ≥6/FAST-ED ≥4 CTA and thrombectomy triggers, labetalol/nicardipine BP-lowering doses, Dartmouth Transfer Center contact, and the neurologic-decline/symptomatic-ICH/angioedema complication pathway. AHA/ASA 2019 Guidelines for Early Management of Acute Ischemic Stroke — general pre-/post-lytic BP targets, alteplase dosing as an accepted alternative, thrombectomy window (0–6h standard/6–24h extended, DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria). Exclusion list is abbreviated for cognitive-load reasons — use the full checklist on the Stroke Box before treating.

05Respiratory Arrest

PULSE PRESENT · NOT BREATHING / AGONAL
NAME
"Pulse present, not breathing — bagging now." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Airway/BVM · pulse checks · IV/naloxone · airway backup — assign aloud.
AIM
Ventilate now, find a reversible cause, and watch the pulse — if it's lost, this becomes a cardiac arrest immediately.
IMMEDIATE SEQUENCE
  • Open the airway; bag-valve-mask with 100% O₂.
  • Rate: 1 breath every 6 seconds (10/min) for a patient with a pulse who isn't breathing — over-ventilating raises intrathoracic pressure and drops cardiac output.
  • Recheck pulse roughly every 2 minutes. If pulse is lost at any point, start compressions immediately and move to the cardiac-arrest card (01/02).
  • Escalate to an advanced airway if BVM is ineffective, ventilation needs to continue for a prolonged period, or the airway needs protecting — see the Intubation Checklist (card 06).
  • Suspect opioid overdose? Give naloxone while continuing to bag — don't let it delay ventilation. IV/IM/IN 0.4–2 mg, may repeat every 2–3 min; higher doses may be needed for potent synthetic opioids. Ventilation is the priority — naloxone is an adjunct, not a substitute for bagging.
SEARCH FOR A CAUSE
Sources: 2020 AHA Guidelines for CPR and ECC (reaffirmed in the 2025 algorithm set) — rescue breathing rate 1 breath/6 sec (10/min) for a patient with a pulse, pulse recheck ~q2min (AHA CPR & ECC Guidelines). Naloxone dosing per standard EM/toxicology references (StatPearls, ACLS.com) — confirm the exact dose/route against LRH's stocked formulary before printing a fixed number into practice.

07Intracranial Hemorrhage

SPONTANEOUS ICH
NAME
"ICH on CT — airway, BP control, and reversal if they're on anticoagulation." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Airway/GCS · BP management · anticoag reversal · neurosurgery call — assign aloud.
AIM
Protect the airway if needed, control BP, reverse anticoagulation fast, and call neurosurgery early.
IMMEDIATE PRIORITIES
  • IV access ×2 large-bore; continuous BP monitoring q5min; repeat neuro checks/GCS q5min.
  • Protect the airway if GCS is low (see card 06 if intubation is needed).
  • Acute BP target: SBP <140 mmHg (AHA/ASA-endorsed range 130–150; avoid driving SBP below ~130 — too aggressive a target hasn't shown benefit and may cause harm). Labetalol 10–20 mg IV q10min PRN, or nicardipine infusion 5 mg/hr, titrate to goal BP.
  • On anticoagulation? Reverse it immediately in parallel — see card 10 (warfarin → PCC/Kcentra or FFP + vitamin K; DOACs → PCC, andexanet alfa no longer commercially available in the US as of Dec 2025; heparin → protamine).
  • STAT non-contrast CT head; coags/platelets/glucose; call neurosurgery early — don't wait for a "final" read.
  • Seizure prophylaxis is not routinely recommended — treat a seizure if one actually occurs, don't pre-load an anticonvulsant "just in case."
  • Signs of herniation/rising ICP: elevate head of bed 30°, consider hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline per your institutional protocol/ENLS reference) as a bridge, and brief hyperventilation only as a temporizing measure while arranging definitive care.
  • Cerebellar hemorrhage >15 mL with deterioration, brainstem compression, or hydrocephalus is a neurosurgical emergency — call immediately, don't wait for a decline.
Sources: Littleton Regional Hospital Intracranial Hemorrhage (ICH) Checklist — SBP <140 mmHg goal, labetalol 10–20 mg IV q10min PRN / nicardipine 5 mg/hr titrated-drip dosing, IV access/monitoring cadence, and reversal-agent-by-anticoagulant framework (see card 10 for current agent-specific doses). 2022 AHA/ASA Guideline for the Management of Spontaneous Intracranial Hemorrhage — acute SBP target range 130–150 (floor ~130), routine seizure prophylaxis not recommended, cerebellar hemorrhage surgical criteria. Hyperosmolar-therapy dosing (mannitol/hypertonic saline) follows the Neurocritical Care Society's ENLS protocol, which the AHA/ASA guideline itself declines to specify a single regimen for — confirm your institution's chosen agent/dose before printing a fixed number.

08Anaphylaxis

EPINEPHRINE IS FIRST-LINE — DON'T DELAY IT
NAME
"This is anaphylaxis — epi IM now." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Epi/IM injection · airway watch · IV fluids · antihistamine/steroid adjuncts — assign aloud.
AIM
IM epinephrine immediately — everything else is an adjunct. Watch the airway if there's angioedema/stridor.
EPINEPHRINE 0.3–0.5 mg IM (1:1000)
anterolateral thigh · repeat q5–15min
DOSES
0
IF NOT RESPONDING TO IM EPI
  • IV fluids: 20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus for hypotension (roughly 1–2 L for an average adult) — repeat as needed.
  • Refractory shock: IV epinephrine infusion, low-dose, by experienced personnel with continuous monitoring — confirm your pharmacy's approved concentration/rate before mixing (~2–10 mcg/min is a commonly cited EM starting range, not a fixed guideline number).
  • On a beta-blocker and not responding to epi? Glucagon 1–5 mg IV slow push (watch for vomiting/aspiration).
  • Positioning: supine with legs elevated if tolerated; sitting if respiratory distress predominates; left-lateral if pregnant.
  • Angioedema/stridor progressing? Low threshold for early, elective, definitive airway — don't wait for severe stridor (see card 06).
ADJUNCTS — NOT FIRST-LINE, DON'T DELAY EPI FOR THESE
  • Diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV — relieves itch/hives only, does not treat airway/hemodynamics.
  • Famotidine 20 mg IV — weak evidence, adjunct only.
  • Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV (or prednisone 1 mg/kg PO) — traditionally given to blunt a biphasic reaction; current evidence on benefit is mixed/controversial per the 2020 update. Reasonable to give, but don't consider it protective on its own.
Observation after treatment: the 2020 update moved away from one universal number — low-risk patients who respond to a single epi dose may be safe after as little as ~1 hour; higher-risk patients (multiple epi doses needed, severe reaction, prior biphasic reaction, asthma, delayed treatment) warrant extended observation up to 6+ hours or admission. About half of biphasic reactions happen in the first 6–12 hours.
Sources: World Allergy Organization Anaphylaxis Guidance 2020; AAAAI/ACAAI/JCAAI Anaphylaxis — 2020 Practice Parameter Update (J Allergy Clin Immunol 2020) — epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IM (1:1000), adult max 0.5 mg, anterolateral thigh, repeat q5–15min; 20 mL/kg fluid bolus; risk-stratified observation guidance. IV epi infusion rate, glucagon dose, and steroid doses are standard EM-reference dosing (WikEM) rather than numbers stated verbatim in the WAO guideline — confirm against your pharmacy's order set.

09Bleeding / Hemorrhagic Shock

CONTROL THE SOURCE · BLOOD OVER CRYSTALLOID
NAME
"Hemorrhagic shock — direct pressure/tourniquet, activate MTP if massive." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Hemorrhage control · IV/IO access ×2 · blood bank/MTP liaison · warming — assign aloud.
AIM
Stop the bleeding, resuscitate with blood not crystalloid, and beat the lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy).
IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT
  • Control external hemorrhage — direct pressure first, then a tourniquet for extremity bleeding not controlled by pressure; wound packing + pressure dressing for junctional wounds.
  • Permissive hypotension (penetrating trauma, no TBI): target SBP ~80–90 mmHg until the source is controlled — don't chase a normal pressure with crystalloid, it dilutes clotting factors and dislodges clot.
  • Concurrent TBI changes the target — do NOT permissively hypotend; keep MAP ≥80 (age-based SBP floor: ~110 for ages 15–49/70+, ~100 for ages 50–69) to protect cerebral perfusion.
  • Resuscitate with balanced blood products, not crystalloid — activate MTP early if massive hemorrhage is anticipated (see card 12).
  • Beat the lethal triad: active warming (warmed fluids/blood, blankets), avoid excess crystalloid (acidosis), correct coagulopathy with balanced products + calcium + TXA.
TRANEXAMIC ACID (TXA) — TIME SINCE INJURY
0:00
Tap START at the time of injury.
Dose: 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g IV over 8 h. Must be given within 3 hours of injury — CRASH-2 showed benefit ≤3h and a signal of harm if given later. Same regimen is used for TBI (CRASH-3), with clearest benefit in mild-moderate TBI (GCS 9–15).
Sources: CRASH-2 Collaborators, Lancet 2010;376(9734):23-32 (TXA 1g/1g regimen, 3-hour window, harm signal after 3h); CRASH-3 Collaborators, Lancet 2019;394(10210):1713-1723 (TBI indication, GCS 9–15 subgroup benefit); StatPearls "Permissive Hypotension" (NBK558915) and "Hemorrhagic Shock" (NBK470382) for shock-class/BP-target guidance. The classic ATLS 4-class shock table is a teaching aid — vitals often don't correlate reliably with actual blood loss, especially in young trauma patients; trend, don't anchor on a single snapshot.

10Reversal of Anticoagulants

LIFE-THREATENING BLEEDING / EMERGENT SURGERY
NAME
"Major bleed on [agent] — reversing now, calling pharmacy." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Identify agent/last dose · pharmacy/hematology call · local hemostasis · recorder — assign aloud.
AIM
Identify the exact agent and last-dose time, involve pharmacy immediately, and reverse — precise dosing is pharmacy-guided.
⚠ Andexanet alfa (Andexxa) was voluntarily withdrawn from the US market in December 2025 after ANNEXA-I showed roughly double the thrombosis rate vs. usual care. 4-factor PCC is now the primary reversal strategy for factor Xa inhibitors — confirm with pharmacy whether any institutional stock remains before considering it.
WARFARIN (VKA)
  • 4F-PCC (Kcentra), weight- and INR-based: INR 2–<4 → 25 units/kg; INR 4–6 → 35 units/kg; INR >6 → 50 units/kg (dosing weight capped at 100 kg).
  • Plus vitamin K 5–10 mg IV, slow infusion over 15–30 min (rapid push risks an anaphylactoid reaction). Vitamin K alone takes 4–6h — always pair with 4F-PCC for emergent reversal.
DABIGATRAN (direct thrombin inhibitor)
  • Idarucizumab (Praxbind) 5 g IV = two 2.5 g/50 mL vials given consecutively.
  • If unavailable: PCC/aPCC 50 units/kg (max 4,000 units) — pharmacy-guided.
FACTOR Xa INHIBITORS (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban)
  • 4F-PCC 50 units/kg — pharmacy-guided; no INR target to titrate to (unlike warfarin).
  • Andexanet alfa is no longer commercially available in the US as of Dec 2025 (see warning above) — do not plan around it unless pharmacy confirms institutional stock.
HEPARIN & LMWH
  • Unfractionated heparin: protamine 1 mg per 100 units of heparin given in the preceding 2–3h, max single dose 50 mg, slow IV (≤5 mg/min) — rapid push risks hypotension/anaphylactoid reaction. Dose decays if more time has passed since the last heparin dose — confirm your institution's time-decay table with pharmacy.
  • Enoxaparin (LMWH): protamine 1 mg per 1 mg enoxaparin if within 8h of last dose; 0.5 mg per 1 mg if 8–12h; generally not indicated beyond 12h. Reversal is never complete for LMWH (max ~60% anti-Xa neutralization).
GENERAL APPROACH
Sources: 2020 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on Management of Bleeding in Patients on Oral Anticoagulants (JACC 2020) — 4F-PCC/vitamin K dosing for warfarin, general approach; idarucizumab FDA label (5g, two 2.5g vials); ISTH SSC 2024 Guidance on Reversal of DOACs. FDA Safety Communication, Andexxa market withdrawal, Dec 2025. Heparin/LMWH protamine dosing per institutional pharmacy references — time-decay tables vary between sources, confirm LRH's own protocol before hardcoding.

11Hyperkalemia — Crash Protocol

K⁺ >6.5 OR ANY LEVEL WITH ECG CHANGES
NAME
"Critical hyperkalemia — calcium first, then shift, then eliminate." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Calcium push · insulin/dextrose · repeat glucose checks · nephrology/dialysis call — assign aloud.
AIM
Stabilize the membrane, shift K⁺ intracellularly, then eliminate it — and get nephrology moving in parallel, not after.
EKG progression: peaked T waves → PR prolongation/P-wave flattening → QRS widening → sine wave (usually K⁺ >9) → VF/asystole. A normal-looking EKG does not exclude imminent arrest — serum K⁺ doesn't correlate tightly with ECG findings.
1 · STABILIZE THE MEMBRANE (does not lower K⁺)
  • Calcium gluconate 10%, 1 g IV over 2–3 min (peripheral-safe) — onset ~1–3 min, duration ~30–60 min. Repeat EKG in 5 min; may repeat calcium if changes persist.
  • Or calcium chloride — more potent per gram but caustic/vesicant; central line strongly preferred.
2 · SHIFT K⁺ INTRACELLULARLY
  • Regular insulin 10 units IV (consider 5 units if insulin-naive/CKD, to reduce hypoglycemia risk) + dextrose — one amp D50 (25g) if glucose <250; onset ~15–30 min, lasts 4–6h.
  • Check glucose at baseline, 30 min, then hourly for 4–6h — hypoglycemia is the main risk of this step.
  • Albuterol nebulized 10–20 mg (4–8× the standard bronchodilator dose) over 10–15 min — adjunct, never monotherapy.
  • Sodium bicarbonate — not recommended as a standalone measure; consider only if significant concurrent metabolic acidosis, and it requires a large volume (situational, not routine).
3 · ELIMINATE K⁺
  • Loop diuretic (furosemide 20–80 mg IV, higher if renal impairment) — only effective with adequate urine output.
  • Newer binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) — slow onset (hours), not for the acute crash; useful to prevent rebound after stabilization.
  • Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate) — controversial, slow, GI-necrosis risk; most current EM guidance recommends against routine ED use.
  • Dialysis = definitive treatment for refractory hyperkalemia, severe/end-stage renal failure, or no response to diuretics. Activate nephrology immediately in parallel — don't wait for medical therapy to "fail" first, especially in known ESRD/dialysis patients.
Sources: ACEP Management Algorithm for the Adult with Hyperkalemia; EMCrit IBCC — Hyperkalemia; LITFL Hyperkalaemia ECG Library (EKG progression). Insulin dose split (5 vs 10 units) and specific glucose-recheck cadence reflect practice-variable, patient-factor-dependent dosing — confirm with your institution's protocol. 14Unstable on the Vent — DOPE

INTUBATED PATIENT ACUTELY DECOMPENSATING
NAME
"Vented patient crashing — taking them off the vent and bagging by hand." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Bagging/airway check · chest exam/needle decompression · vent/circuit check · recorder — assign aloud.
AIM
Disconnect and bag first — it's both diagnostic and therapeutic. Then work D-O-P-E.
Improves with bagging? → the problem was the Equipment (vent/circuit/O₂ source) — fix/swap it, then resume.
No better, or bagging feels tight/hard? → it's patient-related — work Displacement, Obstruction, Pneumothorax below while you keep bagging.
D — DISPLACEMENT
  • Check: auscultate both axillae, chest-rise symmetry, tube depth at the teeth vs. last documented, waveform capnography (flat/decaying = esophageal or dislodged — more reliable than auscultation).
  • Fix: unilateral (usually right mainstem) → deflate cuff, pull back to correct depth, re-secure. Esophageal/fully dislodged → pull it and reintubate immediately, don't waste time.
O — OBSTRUCTION
  • Check: try to pass a suction catheter (can't pass = classic sign of a plugged/kinked tube); look for a visible kink or bite-block failure; auscultate for wheeze vs. no air movement. On the vent: high peak, normal plateau pressure points here.
  • Fix: suction; straighten the kink or remove the bite block; bronchodilators for bronchospasm; if the tube can't be cleared, it's the problem — pull and replace it.
P — PNEUMOTHORAX (esp. tension)
  • Check: unilateral decreased/absent breath sounds, sudden hypotension, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation (late/unreliable sign). On the vent: high peak AND high plateau pressure points here (vs. obstruction, which is peak-only). Classic signs are often subtle in a sedated, monitored patient — keep a high index of suspicion.
  • Fix: if there's hemodynamic compromise, don't wait for imaging — needle decompression (2nd ICS midclavicular, or 4th/5th ICS anterior-axillary) or finger thoracostomy, then a chest tube.
E — EQUIPMENT FAILURE
  • Check: trace the circuit from vent to patient for a disconnection/leak/kink; check O₂ source (wall pressure, tank level); check the vent's power/battery and alarm log.
  • Fix: reconnect/repair the circuit, swap to a backup vent, or replace the O₂ source. Keep hand-bagging with a known-good 100% O₂ source until it's fully resolved and verified.
PEAK vs. PLATEAU PRESSURE — QUICK SORT
  • High peak, normal plateau (<30 cmH₂O): resistance problem → Obstruction (tube kink/plug/bite, bronchospasm, secretions).
  • High peak AND high plateau: compliance problem → Pneumothorax, breath-stacking/auto-PEEP, pulmonary edema, mainstem intubation.
  • Low peak and low plateau: lost circuit pressure/seal → Displacement (extubation/esophageal) or major disconnection.
Sources: UCSF Hospital Handbook, "Troubleshooting the Ventilator" — disconnect-and-bag first step, peak/plateau pressure differentiation; LITFL Critical Care Compendium, "High airway and alveolar pressures." DOPE is standard PALS/ACLS/EM teaching for the crashing ventilated patient; a proposed extension to "DOPES" (adding Stacked breaths) exists in some sources but is not universally adopted — not included here.

15Status Asthmaticus

SEVERE ASTHMA EXACERBATION
NAME
"Severe asthma — nebs and steroids now, BiPAP and intubation on standby." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Nebulizer/med setup · BiPAP initiation · IV access/monitoring · intubation backup ready — assign aloud.
AIM
Bronchodilators and steroids first, escalate early to BiPAP if failing — but don't delay intubation once it's truly indicated.
SEVERITY — ASSESS FIRST
  • Mild: wheezing, tachypnea, speaking in full sentences.
  • Moderate: accessory muscle use, incomplete sentences, SpO₂ <92%.
  • Severe / impending arrest: silent chest, confusion, gasping, altered LOC — a silent chest means too little air is moving to wheeze, not that things are improving.
FIRST-LINE TREATMENT (before BiPAP/intubation)
  • Oxygen: NRB mask at 15 L/min; target SpO₂ >90%.
  • Nebulized albuterol 5 mg q20min ×3, then continuous as needed.
  • Nebulized ipratropium 0.5 mg q20min ×3.
  • IV steroid: methylprednisolone 125 mg IV, or dexamethasone 10 mg IV.
  • Magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over 20 min if refractory.
  • Epinephrine, if severe: IM 0.3–0.5 mg (1:1000) q15min PRN; if hypotensive, IV 10 mcg bolus, then continuous infusion 1–4 mcg/min.
BiPAP SETTINGS (Vision 2000 Ventilator) — if needed before intubation
  • Mode: Spontaneous (S). IPAP: 10–15 cmH₂O (increase to 20 if no improvement). EPAP: 4–6 cmH₂O.
  • Backup RR: 10–14/min. FiO₂: 100% initially, then titrate to SpO₂ >90%. Rise time: 0.2–0.4 sec.
  • Monitor for air trapping/hyperinflation (auto-PEEP). Worsening mental status on BiPAP → prepare to intubate, don't wait for full failure.
INTUBATION — INDICATIONS & APPROACH
  • Indications: persistent hypoxia/hypercapnia despite BiPAP; altered mental status or fatigue; severe acidosis (pH <7.25) or silent chest.
  • Ketamine is the preferred induction agent — bronchodilator effect. 1–2 mg/kg IV bolus for induction; 0.5 mg/kg/hr drip if post-intubation bronchospasm persists (see card 06 for the full airway checklist).
  • Avoid midazolam (can worsen hypercapnia) and propofol alone (can cause hypotension) as the primary sedative here.
  • Watch for breath-stacking/auto-PEEP once vented — if the patient suddenly decompensates on the vent, see card 14 (DOPE).
HEMODYNAMICS & DISPOSITION
  • IV fluids: 250–500 mL boluses if signs of dehydration. Pressors: consider norepinephrine for shock despite fluids.
  • Continuous waveform capnography and pulse oximetry; frequent auscultation for air trapping and worsening CO₂ retention/fatigue.
  • ICU admission if requiring BiPAP or intubation; pulmonology consult for refractory cases.
  • Discharge only if completely symptom-free ≥4 hours post-treatment; admit for continued monitoring if requiring ongoing BiPAP or repeated bronchodilators.

16Angioedema

AIRWAY FIRST · SORT HISTAMINERGIC VS. BRADYKININ
NAME
"Angioedema — checking the airway and sorting histaminergic vs. bradykinin-mediated." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Airway assessment/backup · IV access & meds · identify trigger/type · recorder — assign aloud.
AIM
Protect the airway early — swelling can progress unpredictably. Histaminergic vs. bradykinin-mediated completely changes which drugs work.
ASSESS SEVERITY & TYPE
  • Determine if this is histaminergic (allergic) or bradykinin-mediated (hereditary or ACE-inhibitor-related) — ACE inhibitors are the most common bradykinin trigger.
  • Assess for airway involvement: stridor, voice changes, difficulty swallowing/handling secretions.
  • Identify triggers — medications (especially ACEIs), foods, insect stings, or idiopathic.
AIRWAY — READINESS & EARLY INTUBATION
  • Yankauer plus two functioning suction devices at bedside. Prepare airway tools: video laryngoscope, fiber-optic scope if available, cricothyrotomy kit at bedside. Secure large-bore IV access.
  • Prepare for early intubation if: stridor is present, swelling is progressive, or the voice is muffled/patient can't handle secretions — don't wait for frank respiratory failure.
  • Approach differs from standard RSI: early awake fiber-optic intubation is preferred. Avoid paralysis (RSI) unless mandatory — muscle relaxation can worsen obstruction once spontaneous airway tone is lost. If intubation fails, immediate cricothyrotomy is the backup plan.
HISTAMINERGIC (ALLERGIC) TREATMENT
  • Epinephrine IM 0.3–0.5 mg (1:1000) — repeat q5–15min if needed.
  • Diphenhydramine 50 mg IV. Famotidine 20 mg IV. Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV.
  • If there's systemic involvement (hypotension, urticaria, GI symptoms) this may be anaphylaxis rather than isolated angioedema — see card 08.
BRADYKININ-MEDIATED (ACE-INHIBITOR / HEREDITARY) TREATMENT
  • 🚫 Antihistamines and steroids are not effective here — don't rely on them to buy time.
  • Icatibant 30 mg SC if available.
  • FFP 2 units IV if icatibant unavailable.
  • C1-esterase inhibitor 20 U/kg IV if hereditary angioedema is suspected.
SUPPORTIVE MEASURES & DISPOSITION
  • Nebulized epinephrine (5 mL of 1:1000 solution) if upper airway swelling worsens. IV fluids for hemodynamic stability. Cough suppression may be considered if cough is worsening airway irritation — weigh carefully against respiratory depression risk in a patient with borderline airway.
  • Continuous SpO₂/airway assessment; reassess swelling every 5–10 min. Swelling can worsen even after initial improvement — don't discharge on early improvement alone.
  • Consult ICU for airway monitoring if at risk of delayed swelling; consult ENT or anesthesia for advanced airway support if intubation is anticipated.
  • Admit to ICU for any airway involvement. Discharge only if symptoms fully resolve and the patient has no airway risk factors.

17Tracheostomy Emergency

STEP 1 IS ALWAYS: LARYNGECTOMY OR NOT?
NAME
"Tracheostomy emergency — first question: has this patient had a laryngectomy?" Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Oxygenation/bagging · tube-patency check · airway backup (ENT/anesthesia) · recorder — assign aloud.
AIM
Laryngectomy status changes everything — a laryngectomy patient has no airway above the stoma, so face oxygenation and oral intubation will fail. Confirm it first.
STEP 1 — LARYNGECTOMY STATUS (do this before anything else)
  • Explicitly ask/check: "Has this patient had a laryngectomy?" If unknown, check prior records or examine for normal upper-airway patency.
  • This single fact decides whether face-oxygenation and oral-intubation backup plans will work at all — get the answer before you commit to a plan.
IMMEDIATE OXYGENATION & ASSESSMENT
  • Apply high-flow oxygen to both the face and the stoma until laryngectomy status is confirmed.
  • Assess work of breathing, SpO₂, chest rise, breath sounds, and ability to phonate. Call for backup (ENT/anesthesia/surgical airway) early if deterioration looks likely.
ASSESS TUBE PATENCY & OBSTRUCTION
  • Attempt to pass a suction catheter through the trach tube. Resistance/can't pass → suspect obstruction, mucus plug, or malposition.
  • Determine if the patient is moving any air through the tube. No air movement = complete obstruction — escalate immediately, don't keep troubleshooting in place.
IF THE TUBE IS DISLODGED — CHECK STOMA MATURITY FIRST
  • Stoma <7 days old: do not blindly reinsert — the tract is immature and a false passage is a real risk. Escalate immediately (ENT/surgical airway).
  • Stoma ≥7 days old: carefully attempt tube reinsertion (obturator-guided, appropriate size) while closely monitoring the patient's response.
IF NO LARYNGECTOMY — OXYGENATE/INTUBATE FROM ABOVE
  • Place high-flow O₂ via nasal cannula; attempt oral intubation from above while maintaining oxygenation through the stoma.
  • If unsuccessful, move to tracheostomy tube replacement.
IF LARYNGECTOMY — BAG & INTUBATE THROUGH THE STOMA ONLY
  • Bagging via the face is completely ineffective in a laryngectomy patient — there is no connection to the lower airway. Bag through the stoma.
  • Attempt reintubation through the stoma with an appropriately sized tube. Call ENT or surgical airway backup early.
CONFIRM PLACEMENT & POST-AIRWAY MANAGEMENT
  • Confirm with direct visualization — pass a video scope through the placed tube to confirm tracheal placement and check for distal obstruction. Don't rely on chest rise/SpO₂ alone.
  • Secure the tube, optimize ventilation, reassess frequently, and communicate next steps out loud (ICU admission, bronchoscopy, or surgical intervention) — then debrief.
