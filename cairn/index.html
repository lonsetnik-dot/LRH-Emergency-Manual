Ready for the rare, in every rural ED.

Cairn is an open-source readiness system for rural emergency departments: an interactive emergency manual and team cognitive aids, equipment kit lists and readiness audits, kit indicators, infographics, and simulations you can run with a pillow and fifteen minutes.

A local physician champion takes a branch, keeps the parts that fit, localizes the doses, phone numbers and cart maps, and practices with the team. Then shares what they learned back.

A cairn is a stack of stones left on the trail by those who walked it before you — so you can find the way in bad weather. Add a stone if you can. It's part of the environment now, helping the next traveler the way it helped you.

Walk a case

A precipitous birth becomes a shoulder dystocia becomes a blue baby — three tools handing off to each other. Here's what that handoff looks like.

Illustrative record of the real engines · try the timed, tap-through versions yourself below · not clinical guidance.

SIM 1 · PRECIPITOUS BIRTH
OB · IMMINENT DELIVERY

Head delivers, then stops — turtle sign. Gentle traction fails. Shoulder dystocia declared — the head-to-body clock starts, and it will not restart.

DYSTOCIA ENGINE · HELPERR

One rung at a time — help called, McRoberts, suprapubic pressure — each logged against the running interval. Baby out at 1:38. One tap stamps time of birth; every clock downstream runs off it.

NEONATAL ENGINE · NRP

Floppy, blue, not crying. Warm · dry · stimulate, then PPV in 30-second cycles off the birth clock. HR climbs 50 → 90 → 120 — HR 140, crying, at 9:55. Apgar marks fired on their own.

That record below? Nobody wrote it. The delivery card stamped the clock, the dystocia engine held the interval, the neonatal engine ran the cycles — while the team's hands were busy.

On the floor, teams drill exactly this chain with a pillow and fifteen minutes — it doubles as an equipment walk for new staff.

Why cognitive aids, why rural

A rural ED sees everything an urban trauma center sees — just rarely. When the once-in-three-years case arrives, recall under stress is not a plan. Checklists and practiced teams are.

6% vs 23%

Critical management steps missed in simulated operating-room crises with checklists versus without them.1

~1 in 5

Americans live in rural areas, served by emergency departments that are typically low-volume and far from tertiary backup.2

190+

Rural hospitals closed or converted away from inpatient care since 2005 — the departments that remain carry more, with less.3

Open source, openly governed

Everything lives in one repository under a permissive license: the manual, the cognitive aids, the kit lists, the label and poster templates, the simulation scripts, and the guideline-provenance records that say which source each tool is written against and when a human last checked it.

Adoption starts with an introduction: reach out, and we hand you the repository along with the people who maintain it. From there it's a fork — your copy, your hosting, your clinical validation. No patient data, ever, by design.

Governance is being built

Clinical review panel, co-maintainers, contribution pathways — the structures are forming now, and early voices shape them. If you want a say in how a shared rural readiness commons is run, this is the moment.

Cairn · cairnready.org · open source

Cairn is a cognitive aid, not a source of truth you inherit unverified. It is not part of any hospital IT system or EHR, not a substitute for clinical judgment, and not an FDA-cleared device. Every adopting site is responsible for validating all content — every dose, threshold, and protocol — against its own assays, formulary, equipment, and medical direction before clinical use. No patient data is stored or transmitted, by design.

Built in the open by rural emergency clinicians, for each other.
