01Cardiac Arrest / PALS

SHOCKABLE VS. NON-SHOCKABLE · WEIGHT-BASED
NAME
"Pediatric cardiac arrest — starting compressions, weight is [X] kg." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Compressions · airway/ventilation · IV-IO access/meds · weight entry & defib — assign aloud.
AIM
High-quality compressions, early epinephrine for non-shockable rhythms, and get a weight entered above so every dose below is correct.
Enter the patient's weight in the bar at the top of the page — it stays set as you move between cards and every calculated dose on this page updates from it live. If weight is genuinely unknown, use the age-based estimate button, understanding it's an estimate, not a substitute for an actual weight/length measurement when one can be obtained. (2025 FDA Class I recall of the Broselow Pediatric Emergency Tape's 2025 edition — incorrect drug/energy values printed in three color zones — is part of why this tool computes directly from an entered weight rather than a printed length-tape lookup.)
COMPRESSIONS & VENTILATION
  • Rate 100–120/min, all pediatric ages. Depth ≥⅓ AP chest diameter — ~4 cm infants, ~5 cm children; post-puberty use adult depth (≥5 cm, ≤6 cm).
  • No advanced airway: 30:2 single rescuer (all ages); 15:2 with two rescuers, pre-pubertal child/infant.
  • With advanced airway: continuous compressions + 1 breath every 2–3 sec (20–30/min), not synchronized with compressions.
  • Infant compressions: heel-of-one-hand or two-thumb-encircling technique (2025 update removed the two-finger technique).

NON-SHOCKABLE (asystole / PEA)
  • CPR + give epinephrine as early as possible — the 2025 update strengthened this from "within 5 min" to as-early-as-possible; outcomes are better when it's given within ~3 min.

AIRWAY SIZING (age-based, not weight-based)
  • Uncuffed ETT internal diameter (mm) ≈ (age in years ÷ 4) + 4. Cuffed ≈ same − 0.5 mm.
  • Depth at the lip (cm) ≈ 3 × internal tube diameter (mm).
  • These are widely-taught airway-literature formulas rather than a numbered line in the open-access AHA guideline text — always confirm tube position with waveform capnography + CXR regardless of the formula used.
2025 UPDATES WORTH KNOWING
  • With an invasive arterial line during CPR, target diastolic BP ≥25 mmHg (infants), ≥30 mmHg (children ≥1 yr).
  • No single ETCO₂ cutoff should be used alone to decide to stop resuscitation.
  • Post-arrest: keep systolic/mean BP above the 10th percentile for age.
  • No pediatric intra-arrest termination-of-resuscitation checklist exists — see card 13 for the broader pediatric-death framework.

02Anaphylaxis

EPINEPHRINE IS FIRST-LINE — DON'T DELAY IT
NAME
"This is anaphylaxis — epi IM now." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Epi/IM injection · airway watch · IV fluids · antihistamine/steroid adjuncts — assign aloud.
AIM
IM epinephrine immediately — everything else is an adjunct. Watch the airway if there's angioedema/stridor.
EPINEPHRINE — CALCULATED FROM WEIGHT ABOVE
RouteDoseMaxRepeat
IM (1:1000), anterolateral thigh0.01 mg/kg = enter weight above0.5 mg (WAO 2020)q5–15min
The 2023 AAAAI/ACAAI update caps the pediatric/teen dose at 0.3 mg instead of 0.5 mg — the two major sources disagree on the ceiling; this tool uses the WAO 2020 figure (0.5 mg) but confirm which cap your pharmacy/EMS protocol uses. Auto-injector fixed doses (for reference, not a substitute for a weight-based calculation when you can draw one up): 0.1 mg for 7.5–15 kg, 0.15 mg for 15–30 kg, 0.3 mg for ≥30 kg — some bodies now favor switching to the 0.3 mg device closer to 25 kg to avoid underdosing.
IF NOT RESPONDING TO IM EPI
  • IV fluids: 20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus = enter weight above (max 1 L/dose) — repeat as needed for persistent hypotension.
  • Refractory shock: IV epinephrine infusion, low-dose, by experienced personnel with continuous monitoring — confirm your pharmacy's concentration/rate before mixing.
  • Angioedema/stridor progressing? Low threshold for early, elective, definitive airway — don't wait for severe stridor.
ADJUNCTS — CALCULATED FROM WEIGHT ABOVE — NOT FIRST-LINE, DON'T DELAY EPI FOR THESE

These relieve itch/hives (antihistamine) or are traditionally given to blunt a biphasic reaction (steroid) — none of them treat the airway/hemodynamic compromise. They come from institutional pediatric ED pathways, not a single graded national-guideline dosing table.
Observation after treatment: a large 2025 pediatric ED cohort (5,641 patients, 31 sites) found only 4.7% needed repeat epinephrine after 2 hours and 1.9% after 4 hours — suggesting 2 hours is likely sufficient for children without cardiovascular involvement, 4 hours for those with cardiovascular symptoms. Older institutional pathways still specify a flat 4–6 hour observation; a small rural ED without close follow-up access may reasonably choose the more conservative standard.

03Asthma Exacerbation

DEXAMETHASONE-FIRST · PASS SEVERITY
NAME
"Pediatric asthma exacerbation — [mild/moderate/severe], starting weight-based albuterol." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Nebulizer/MDI setup · dexamethasone · IV access if severe · monitoring — assign aloud.
AIM
Determine PASS severity first — it decides everything downstream. Dexamethasone up front, weight-based albuterol, escalate fast if severe.
SEVERITY (PASS) & TRIAGE
  • Mild: ESI 4 — dexamethasone tablet + albuterol MDI 2–4 puffs, MDI spacer teaching, discharge and assess asthma control.
  • Moderate: ESI 3 — dexamethasone tablet + weight-based albuterol nebulizer q20min ×3 PRN. Reassess: allow ≥2h for steroid effect before disposition.
  • Severe: ESI 1–2 — dexamethasone tablet (alt: IM dex/IV methylprednisolone) + combined albuterol/ipratropium nebulizer; if unable to tolerate a mask, MDI with albuterol+ipratropium instead. Consider IV magnesium; consider subQ terbutaline.
  • Critical/impending respiratory failure: to resuscitation room — escalation medications, initiate NIPPV at 10/5 cmH₂O and titrate with RT; if no improvement, prepare for intubation and trigger a Tier 1 PICU alert/transfer.
  • Supplemental O₂ for persistent SpO₂ <90%.

04Airway Obstruction (Choking)

FOREIGN BODY ASPIRATION
NAME
"Choking — complete/partial obstruction, [age]." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
BLS maneuvers · airway equipment/Magill forceps ready · surgical airway backup — assign aloud.
AIM
Effective cough = don't intervene, watch closely. Ineffective/absent cough = act immediately, technique differs by age.
PARTIAL VS. COMPLETE OBSTRUCTION
  • Effective cough / good air movement: do not intervene — let the child cough, monitor closely, prepare for escalation, and consider bronchoscopy referral even without positive imaging if aspiration is genuinely suspected.
  • Weak/absent cough, cyanosis, stridor, poor air movement (complete obstruction): intervene immediately per age-based technique below.
  • Unresponsive, any age: start CPR (chest compressions); before rescue breaths, open the mouth and look for a visible object to remove. Blind finger sweeps are not recommended unless the object is directly visualized.
TECHNIQUE BY AGE (2025 AHA/AAP update)
  • Infant <1 yr: alternate 5 back blows (prone along your forearm, head lower than chest) with 5 chest thrusts (two fingers, lower half of sternum). Abdominal thrusts are contraindicated in infants — liver/spleen injury risk.
  • Child ≥1 yr (and adults): alternate 5 back blows with 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich). This is a 2020–2025 change from older "abdominal-thrusts-only" teaching — if you trained before that, note the addition of back blows to the child sequence.
  • Continue cycling until the object is expelled or the victim becomes unresponsive.
IF BLS MANEUVERS FAIL — PROVIDER ESCALATION
  • Direct laryngoscopy with Magill forceps or suction to retrieve a visualized object above the cords.
  • If the object is at/below the cords and removal fails → needle cricothyrotomy with transtracheal jet ventilation is the consistent recommendation for young children.
  • The age/size cutoff for surgical vs. needle cricothyrotomy is genuinely unsettled in the literature (sources vary roughly 5–12 years) — the cricothyroid membrane is extremely small in young children, so anatomic size, not a fixed age, is probably the more important variable. Don't rely on a single hard cutoff number.
Sources: AHA/AAP 2025 Guidelines for CPR and ECC, Part 6: Pediatric BLS, Circulation 2025 (CIR.0000000000001370) — back-blow/thrust technique by age, contraindication of abdominal thrusts in infants; American Red Cross FBAO Guidelines Database. StatPearls, Foreign Body Airway Obstruction and Cricothyroidotomy; WikEM and LITFL, needle cricothyrotomy age/size discussion — presented as an area of genuine guideline disagreement, not a fixed cutoff.

05Epiglottitis / Upper Airway Emergency

DO NOT AGITATE · DO NOT EXAMINE THE AIRWAY
NAME
"Possible epiglottitis — keeping the child calm with the parent, calling for airway backup now." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Minimal-handling protocol · blow-by O₂ · transfer team call · surgical airway kit at bedside — assign aloud.
AIM
Disturb the least, prepare for the worst. Don't touch the airway or agitate the child — call for transport and an airway team in parallel.
Do not lay the child flat. Do not place an IV. Do not examine the oropharynx with a tongue depressor. Do not force a mask onto the face. Any of these can precipitate sudden complete obstruction via a ball-valve effect.
CLASSIC PRESENTATION
  • Abrupt-onset high fever, toxic/anxious appearance, tripod/"sniffing" positioning (sitting forward, neck extended, chin protruding), drooling, muffled "hot potato" voice, inspiratory stridor, refusal to swallow.
RURAL ED — WHAT TO DO WHILE AWAITING TRANSFER
  • Keep the child sitting up with the parent, in whatever position is most comfortable to the child. Blow-by humidified O₂ as tolerated — not a mask forced onto the face.
  • Call the regional children's hospital/PICU transport team and the receiving ED immediately, in parallel with everything else. Ask whether their airway team should come to you or advise on securing the airway before transport.
  • Have a pediatric-sized surgical/cricothyrotomy kit opened and a BVM ready at bedside — but do not attempt laryngoscopy/intubation unless the child is in extremis (peri-arrest).
  • Monitor continuously without repeated exams. Do not send the child to CT.
  • No single named guideline exists specifically for a rural ED without in-house ENT/anesthesia — this is a synthesis of the general "disturb the least, prepare for the worst" principle for a facility that would normally secure this airway in an OR with ENT and anesthesia present.
ANTIBIOTICS ONCE AIRWAY IS SECURED
  • Immunocompetent: ceftriaxone 50–100 mg/kg/day IV (max 2 g/day); alternatives cefotaxime or ampicillin-sulbactam. Add vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q6h if MRSA is a concern.
  • Immunocompromised: cefepime 50 mg/kg IV q8h (max 2 g) + vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV q6h.
  • Epidemiology has shifted since the Hib vaccine: today's cases are more often S. pneumoniae, group A/C strep, S. aureus, M. catarrhalis, or non-typeable H. influenzae — don't be falsely reassured by Hib vaccination status.

06Bronchiolitis

SUPPORTIVE CARE — NOT ASTHMA, DON'T TREAT IT LIKE ASTHMA
NAME
"Bronchiolitis — supportive care, watching for respiratory failure." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Suction · oxygen/HFNC · hydration · intubation backup if apneic — assign aloud.
AIM
Supportive care is the treatment — bronchodilators/epi/steroids don't work here. Watch closely for apnea/respiratory failure, especially in young infants.
Bronchiolitis is a clinical diagnosis in a typical presentation (viral prodrome, then increased work of breathing with crackles/wheeze in an infant <24 months) — routine labs and chest imaging are not needed in a straightforward case and mainly add noise/false reassurance.
SUPPORTIVE CARE (what actually works)
  • Nasal suctioning — often the single most helpful intervention; infants are obligate nasal breathers.
  • Supplemental oxygen to a target saturation of roughly ≥90% — chasing a normal-adult saturation target is not necessary and can prolong admission without benefit.
  • Hydration: support oral intake if tolerated; IV or NG fluids if not — a crystalloid bolus (push-pull technique with a 3-way stopcock in small infants) if there's evidence of significant dehydration/shock.
  • Antipyretics (acetaminophen) for fever/comfort as needed.
  • Escalate to high-flow nasal cannula or NIPPV for increasing work of breathing/hypoxia not responding to low-flow O₂ and suctioning.
WHAT DOESN'T WORK — DON'T TREAT THIS LIKE ASTHMA
  • Bronchodilators (albuterol/salbutamol), nebulized epinephrine, and ipratropium — may transiently bump heart rate but do not meaningfully change work of breathing or oxygen saturation in true bronchiolitis; not routinely recommended.
  • Systemic or inhaled corticosteroids — not effective in bronchiolitis (distinct from the asthma pathway on card 03).
  • Chest physiotherapy and hypertonic saline nebs — not routinely recommended in the ED setting.
  • If a bronchodilator trial is given and there's no real change in work of breathing, don't keep re-dosing it hoping for a different result — that's a sign this is bronchiolitis, not asthma.
WATCH FOR RESPIRATORY FAILURE
  • Young infants (especially <3 months, ex-preemies, or those with underlying cardiopulmonary disease) can progress to apnea with little warning — this can be the presenting/worsening sign rather than a late one.
  • Have intubation equipment and weight-based airway/med doses ready (see card 01) any time an infant with bronchiolitis is on your radar for concern — apnea can happen abruptly even after a period of apparent stability on oxygen.
  • Once intubated for bronchiolitis-associated respiratory failure, call the receiving PICU immediately and arrange transfer.
Sources: Friedman JN, Rieder MJ, Walton JM; Canadian Paediatric Society, Bronchiolitis: Recommendations for diagnosis, monitoring and management of children one to 24 months of age, 2021 (cps.ca/en/documents/position/bronchiolitis) — supportive-care-only framework, discouraging bronchodilators/steroids/chest physio/routine hypertonic saline, and the SpO₂ target discussion. Bronchiolitis-vs-asthma distinction and apnea/respiratory-failure progression pattern cross-checked against a University of British Columbia Department of Emergency Medicine simulation case (Salehmohamed, 2022), used here only as a teaching-scenario cross-check, not as an independent clinical source.

07Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

SLOW, STEADY CORRECTION · WATCH FOR CEREBRAL EDEMA
NAME
"Suspected pediatric DKA — starting fluids, watching for cerebral edema." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
2 PIV access · fluids/insulin timing · hourly glucose/q2h labs · neuro checks — assign aloud.
AIM
Rehydrate first, start insulin only after the first bolus, correct slowly (50–100 mg/dL/hr glucose drop), and watch for cerebral edema the whole time.
Cerebral edema occurs in 0.3–0.9% of pediatric DKA with 21–24% mortality, and can appear before, during, or after treatment (typically within 12h). Watch for new headache, recurrent vomiting, altered mental status, urinary incontinence, or a Cushing triad (bradycardia, irregular respirations, hypertension) — treat empirically on high suspicion, don't wait for imaging.
DIAGNOSIS & INITIAL ASSESSMENT
  • DKA confirmed: glucose >200 mg/dL AND beta-hydroxybutyrate >3 mmol/L AND (pH <7.3 OR HCO₃ <15 mmol/L).
  • 2 peripheral IVs; initial labs: VBG, BMP, Mg, phosphate, HgbA1c, CBC, HCG as indicated. POC glucose and POC beta-hydroxybutyrate at triage.
  • Assess mental status, vital signs, dehydration, and cerebral edema risk from the start — start an ED nursing care map/flowsheet.
FLUIDS — CALCULATED FROM WEIGHT ABOVE
  • Initial bolus: 20 mL/kg NS over 1 hour = enter weight above. Additional NS/LR boluses as indicated by exam/vitals.
  • Then start maintenance at 1.5× maintenance rate until electrolyte results are back (NS 1.5×maintenance, no dextrose yet unless glucose is dropping — see below).
  • Insulin starts only after the first NS bolus is completed — never before.

INSULIN & MONITORING
  • Regular insulin infusion 0.1 units/kg/hr = enter weight above, started after the initial NS bolus is complete. Stop a home insulin pump; review home Lantus dosing with endocrinology.
  • Labs: POC blood glucose hourly; BMP/Mg/phos and POC beta-hydroxybutyrate q2h; VBG q2h until pH >7.0.
  • Monitoring: full cardiorespiratory monitoring; vitals and neuro assessment hourly.
  • Resolution of DKA: mental status/vitals improved, tolerating sips, HCO₃ ≥15 mmol/L (or beta-hydroxybutyrate <2 mmol/L), anion gap <10 → transition to subcutaneous insulin (give first rapid-acting SC dose, confirm Lantus given, allow eating, wait 20 min, then stop the infusion/IVF).
SMALL RURAL ED — ICU ADMISSION / TRANSFER
  • Most pediatric DKA (especially first presentation, young age, severe acidosis, or altered mental status) warrants ICU-level care — if LRH doesn't have pediatric ICU capability, start the bolus/insulin protocol above and arrange transfer early rather than trying to manage the full multi-hour correction locally.
  • If cerebral edema is suspected, treat first (hypertonic saline or mannitol per your institutional protocol, head of bed elevation) — don't wait for transfer or imaging to act.

ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetic Ketoacidosis and Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (cerebral edema epidemiology/warning signs, cited within the CHOP pathway's own evidence page).

08Altered Mental Status

GLUCOSE FIRST · DON'T MISS THE RED FLAGS
NAME
"Altered mental status — checking glucose first." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Airway/GCS · glucose/dextrose · naloxone if suspected opioid · red-flag workup — assign aloud.
AIM
Airway/breathing/circulation, then glucose immediately. Work the differential fast — several causes (hypoglycemia, meningitis, abusive head trauma) are time-critical.
INITIAL STABILIZATION
  • ABCs first — GCS ≤8 or non-protective airway reflexes → intubate (see card 01/06).
  • Point-of-care glucose immediately, before or alongside everything else. Treat if <60 mg/dL (<40 mg/dL in neonates); an awake/alert child can take oral glucose instead of IV dextrose.
DEXTROSE DOSING — CALCULATED FROM WEIGHT ABOVE ("5/2/1–50" rule)

Each row delivers the same "glucose dose" regardless of which concentration is on hand (5×10 = 2×25 = 1×50). D10/D25 preferred over D50 peripherally in children — extravasation/phlebitis risk. This is a widely-used educational convention, not a numbered society-guideline value — sensible but not independently graded evidence.
NALOXONE IF OPIOID EXPOSURE SUSPECTED
  • Neonates: 0.01 mg/kg IV/IM/SC, repeat q2–3min.
  • Children: 0.01 mg/kg IV initially; may increase to 0.1 mg/kg IV if inadequate response. Auto-injector/IM fixed dose: 0.4 mg.
  • Sources genuinely conflict here — an older (1990) AAP addendum used a flat 0.1 mg/kg starting dose for resuscitation, while current pharmacy references favor titrating from a lower 0.01 mg/kg starting dose to avoid precipitating withdrawal in a chronically-exposed child. For a clear overdose/respiratory-arrest presentation, don't under-dose out of caution — confirm your current tox reference before finalizing an exact number.
RED FLAGS TO RULE OUT FAST
The mnemonic AEIOU-TIPS (alcohol/acidosis, epilepsy/electrolytes, insulin, overdose, uremia, trauma, infection, psychiatric/poisoning, stroke/shock/seizure) is widely taught for organizing this differential — it's an educational convention, not an issued guideline.
Sources: Song JL, Wang VJ, "Altered Level of Consciousness in the Pediatric Patient," Pediatric Emergency Medicine Practice, EB Medicine, Jan 2017 (AEIOU-TIPS, GCS≤8 intubation threshold, initial-stabilization sequence); PEMBlog, "5/2/1–50" dextrose rule; AAP Technical Report, "Abusive Head Trauma in Infants and Children," Pediatrics 2025;155(3):e2024070457; ISPAD 2018 DKA/cerebral-edema criteria; Drugs.com pediatric naloxone dosing, cross-checked against the 1990 AAP addendum on naloxone dosing (conflict noted above — verify against a current pharmacy reference before printing a fixed number).

09Behavioral Agitation

DE-ESCALATE FIRST · MEDS ARE THE FALLBACK, NOT THE FIRST MOVE
NAME
"Acute agitation — de-escalating first, low-stimulation space." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Verbal de-escalation lead · environment/family liaison · medication if needed · safety/monitoring — assign aloud.
AIM
Non-pharmacologic de-escalation is first-line, attempted before, during, and after any medication — never simply replaced by it. Restraint is a last resort.
DE-ESCALATION FIRST
  • Neutral/empathic tone, eye-level communication, clear/concrete/simple language, reflective listening and validation.
  • Move to a low-stimulation, sharps-free space; offer food, comfort items, sensory kits.
  • Autism/neurodevelopmental presentations: get history from the parent/guardian first — sensory overload, pain, GI distress, and unmet communication needs are common non-behavioral triggers. Preferred first pharmacologic step, if needed, is an extra dose of the patient's own home standing medication rather than a new unfamiliar agent. Avoid IM administration unless there's an immediate safety threat — this population is particularly vulnerable to adverse drug events, including from benzodiazepines.
IF MEDICATION IS NEEDED — ORAL BEFORE IM WHEN SAFE
  • Diphenhydramine: 1 mg/kg PO/IM, max ~50 mg, q6h. Avoid if agitation looks like delirium — can worsen it.
  • Hydroxyzine (age-banded): 12.5 mg (5–10y), 25 mg (11–15y), 50 mg (16y+), q6–8h. QT-prolongation caution.
  • Lorazepam: 0.05–0.1 mg/kg PO/IV/IM (≈0.5–2 mg), q4–6h. Avoid in suspected delirium — paradoxical disinhibition risk.
  • Olanzapine: roughly 2.5–5 mg (<45 kg) to 5–10 mg (≥45 kg) PO/IM. Do not give IM olanzapine and IM benzodiazepine within 1 hour of each other — additive sedation/respiratory depression.
  • Risperidone PO: ~0.25 mg (<22 kg) to 0.5–1 mg (≥45 kg); watch for akathisia at higher doses.
  • Haloperidol: use cautiously — ~0.025–0.075 mg/kg/dose PO (children 0.5–2 mg; adolescents 2–5 mg), IM dose = half the oral dose. Avoid IV haloperidol. Co-administer diphenhydramine to reduce dystonic-reaction risk; QT monitoring required.
  • 🚫 Ketamine and barbiturates are not recommended for behavioral-agitation management in the pediatric consensus statement (distinct from their use in acute severe/violent adult-EM agitation protocols) — don't reach for these here without institutional sign-off.
Exact mg dosing varies across secondary institutional guidelines even when all cite the same consensus statement — treat these as expert-opinion-based, not RCT-derived, and confirm against your pharmacy's approved pediatric reference before printing a fixed number.
PHYSICAL RESTRAINT — LAST RESORT, AGE-BASED TIME LIMITS
  • Restraint order renewal (violent/self-destructive behavior): every 1 hour for children <9y, every 2 hours for 9–17y, every 4h for adults ≥18y.
  • A licensed independent practitioner must conduct a face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour of restraint initiation, and personally reassess at every renewal; a face-to-face reassessment is required at least every 24 hours regardless of renewals.
  • Document: date/time of initiation, less-restrictive alternatives attempted, rationale, and exact date/time of discontinuation.
  • Continuous monitoring for any child who has received multiple sedating doses — watch for cumulative respiratory depression, akathisia, and neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

≥38.0°C · TERM, WELL-APPEARING, 8–60 DAYS
NAME
"Febrile infant, [X] days old — starting the age-based workup." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Cultures/UA · empiric antibiotics if indicated · transfer coordination · recorder — assign aloud.
AIM
Age band decides the workup. Any infant <8 days or ill-appearing at any age gets the full workup regardless of these bands.
This card covers well-appearing, term (≥37 weeks) infants 8–60 days old with fever ≥38.0°C (100.4°F) and no focal source. Infants <8 days old, and any ill-appearing infant of any age, are outside this pathway — treat as high-risk: full workup, empiric antibiotics, admit.

"ABNORMAL" INFLAMMATORY MARKER THRESHOLDS (AAP 2021 CPG)
  • Temperature ≥38.5°C (used specifically in the 22–28 day algorithm), CRP >20 mg/L, procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL, ANC >4,000/mm³ (or >5,200/mm³ if procalcitonin unavailable).
  • Any one abnormal marker triggers escalation of workup for that band.
  • For 61–90 days (outside the AAP 2021 CPG's own scope), the PECARN rule uses different cutoffs — don't mix thresholds between algorithms on the same case.
EMPIRIC ANTIBIOTICS, 8–28 DAYS
  • Ampicillin + gentamicin (or ampicillin + cefotaxime/ceftazidime if an aminoglycoside is undesirable) — covers GBS, E. coli, Listeria. Add acyclovir empirically if HSV risk factors are present (maternal HSV history, vesicular rash, seizures, CSF pleocytosis with negative Gram stain, ill appearance).
  • Ampicillin (meningitis dosing): ≤7 days old — 100 mg/kg/dose IV q8h; >7 days old — 75 mg/kg/dose IV q6h.
  • Gentamicin dosing is gestational-age- and postnatal-age-dependent (roughly 4–5 mg/kg per dose, interval ranging q24h to q48h depending on prematurity) — this is a poor candidate for a single static bedside number; get pharmacy/NICU input rather than printing one fixed interval, especially for the smallest/most premature infants.
  • 🚫 Avoid ceftriaxone in neonates/young infants — bilirubin-displacement/kernicterus risk, and never co-administer with IV calcium-containing solutions in neonates (fatal precipitation reported). Ampicillin + gentamicin/cefotaxime is the safer default ≤28 days.
  • 29–60 days, outpatient route (e.g. UTI): ceftriaxone IV/IM, or oral cephalexin/cefixime, is acceptable at this older age.
SMALL RURAL ED — NO LOCAL LP/CSF CAPABILITY
  • The AAP guideline's own logic: if LP is unsuccessful/traumatic/deferred, treat as if the CSF result were positive (empiric antibiotics + admission) until proven otherwise. The same logic extends to a setting where LP simply isn't feasible.
  • Draw blood/urine cultures if it doesn't meaningfully delay transfer, give the first empiric antibiotic dose promptly — don't wait for transfer to start therapy — and arrange transfer to a tertiary pediatric center rather than attempting definitive local management for any infant ≤28 days or any ill-appearing infant of any age.
  • This stabilize-and-transfer approach is a reasonable extrapolation of the AAP guideline's own principle, not a separately published rural-specific guideline — worth review/sign-off from LRH's pediatric/ED medical director before treating it as formal protocol. Consider pediatric tele-EM consultation if available.

12Non-Accidental Trauma

SUSPECTED CHILD PHYSICAL ABUSE
NAME
"This injury pattern is concerning for abuse — starting the standard workup and involving social work." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Exam/documentation · imaging orders · social work/CPS liaison · law enforcement if indicated — assign aloud.
AIM
"Those who don't cruise rarely bruise." Any bruising/injury in a non-mobile infant deserves a full workup, not reassurance — document objectively, report, and let a comprehensive workup rule things in or out.
RED FLAGS — TEN-4-FACESp (children <4 years)
  • TEN-4: any bruising to the Torso, Ears, or Neck in an infant 4 months or younger is a red flag, regardless of how minor it looks — a pre-mobile infant has no mechanism to bruise itself accidentally.
  • FACESp: bruising to the Frenulum, Angle of the jaw, fleshy Cheeks, Eyelids, or Subconjunctivae, or any patterned bruise (loop marks, hand/slap marks, ligature/bite marks) in a child under 4 years.
  • This is a screening tool, not a diagnosis — any single positive finding should trigger referral for a full evaluation, not an accusation.
  • Sentinel injuries: a prior "minor" injury (bruise, intraoral injury) in a non-mobile infant with an implausible explanation is strongly associated with confirmed abuse on later evaluation — don't dismiss a reported prior injury as unrelated.
STANDARD WORKUP WHEN NAT IS SUSPECTED
  • Skeletal survey — routine for suspected abuse under age 2 (extend to age 5 in select cases: developmental delay, nonverbal child, multiple/pattern injuries). Must be a full dedicated survey (~19+ views), not a single babygram. Repeat at ~2 weeks to catch healing fractures not visible acutely.
  • Head imaging: non-contrast CT is first-line for acute triage — any infant <6 months with any abuse finding, <12 months with head/facial bruising, or any age with neuro signs (including "soft" signs — vomiting, irritability, lethargy) without a plausible accidental mechanism. MRI is added as an adjunct (usually within days) to characterize injury timing and screen the spine.
  • Retinal exam — for children <5 years with unexplained coma/seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, or unexplained death; full dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy by an ophthalmologist, ideally within 24h (no later than 72h) since hemorrhages can resolve quickly. Not required for straightforward abuse evaluations without neuroimaging findings.
  • Coagulation studies — CBC with platelets, PT, PTT (± von Willebrand panel) to rule out a bleeding-disorder mimic before concluding non-accidental etiology, whenever bruising or intracranial hemorrhage is the basis for concern.
  • This inverts PECARN's usual logic — PECARN's accidental-trauma rule is designed to limit unnecessary CT in low-risk, plausible-mechanism injury. Once abuse is suspected, imaging becomes presumptive/comprehensive regardless of whether the reported mechanism would otherwise meet PECARN's "low risk" criteria, because the mechanism itself is what's in question. Low threshold to admit once NAT is genuinely suspected.
REPORTING & DOCUMENTATION
  • New Hampshire law (RSA 169-C:29-31, verify current text with hospital legal/social work): any person who suspects abuse/neglect of a child under 18 must report immediately to NH DCYF Central Intake — proof is not required, reports can be anonymous. A written report may be requested within 48 hours.
  • Involve hospital social work immediately for the CPS liaison; law enforcement is typically notified by social work/CPS or per state law when the injury is suspicious for a crime. Obtain a child-abuse-pediatrics consult (in-person or telehealth) when available.
  • Documentation: record verbatim history from each caregiver separately (and the child if verbal) in quotation marks with attribution; use objective, descriptive language for injuries (size/shape/color/anatomic location) — avoid conclusory phrases like "bruise consistent with abuse" in the objective exam. Photograph with a distance progression (identification → regional overview with landmarks → close-up with a scale), consistent lighting, before cleaning when possible.

13Death of a Child in the ED

NOTIFICATION · FAMILY PRESENCE · LOGISTICS
NAME
"We need to notify the family — gathering the team and chaplaincy first." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Family notification lead · chaplaincy/social work · ME/OPO notification · staff debrief coordination — assign aloud.
AIM
Use the word "died," not "passed" or "didn't make it." Notify the medical examiner and organ-procurement organization for every death — these are not optional or family-permission-gated.
FAMILY NOTIFICATION — GRIEV_ING
  • Gather the family in a private space. Resources — call chaplain/social work before entering. Identify yourself and the deceased by name.
  • Educate — walk through what was done, avoid euphemisms. Verify — use the words "died"/"dead," not "passed" or "didn't make it." Then pause and give space for the reaction.
  • Inquire about questions and whether they want to view the body. Nuts and bolts — autopsy, medical examiner involvement, belongings, next steps. Give your contact info for follow-up.
  • This mnemonic was developed for general ED death notification, not pediatric-specific — adapt tone accordingly, but the structure holds.
FAMILY PRESENCE DURING RESUSCITATION
  • Family presence should be offered as an option per institutional policy — evidence shows no prolongation of resuscitation time and no decreased team communication, and it is rarely disruptive.
  • Assign a dedicated support person (not a clinical team member) to stay with the family the whole time, explain what's happening, and be ready to escort them out if needed.
TERMINATION OF RESUSCITATION — NO FIXED PEDIATRIC RULE
  • Unlike adults, no validated pediatric termination-of-resuscitation rule exists for medical arrests — this is team/attending clinical judgment informed by arrest etiology, downtime, and response to resuscitation, not a checklist.
  • A joint AAP/ACEP/NAEMSP policy statement does address traumatic out-of-hospital pediatric arrest specifically — pull that document directly if this is a trauma arrest, since its criteria are more defined than for medical arrests.

14Procedural Sedation

NPO STATUS NO LONGER GATES SEDATION
NAME
"Procedural sedation for [procedure] — monitoring and airway equipment ready first." Say it out loud.
CLAIM
Proceduralist · dedicated monitoring person (no other duties) · airway/rescue equipment ready — assign aloud.
AIM
Two-person minimum — one proceduralist, one monitoring only. Fasting time doesn't need to delay you; monitoring and a rescue plan do the safety work.
PRE-SEDATION — MONITORING & PERSONNEL, NOT A FASTING CLOCK
  • Do not delay sedation based on NPO/fasting time — ACEP's current clinical policy found no association between fasting duration and aspiration/major complications in a 139,142-sedation cohort. If you want a caution flag, tie it to real aspiration risk factors (obesity, OSA, recent large solid meal + deep sedation with a non-ketamine agent), not a fixed clock.
  • Monitoring: continuous pulse oximetry and cardiac monitoring for all moderate/deep sedation; capnography required for deep sedation, recommended for moderate.
  • Personnel: minimum two people — one proceduralist, one PALS-trained person with no other duties whose sole job is monitoring. Vitals roughly q10min (moderate sedation) or q5min (deep sedation).
  • Resuscitation/airway rescue equipment and reversal agents (naloxone, flumazenil) immediately available before starting.

By procedure: fracture/dislocation reduction → IV ketamine alone, ketamine+propofol ("ketofol"), or propofol+fentanyl. Ketamine is relatively contraindicated under 3 months (higher laryngospasm risk); it preserves airway reflexes and is favored if unfasted or in reactive airway disease, and does not raise ICP.
COMPLICATIONS
  • Laryngospasm (most associated with ketamine, especially <3 months or active/recent URI): jaw thrust/repositioning, 100% O₂, positive-pressure BVM; if refractory, bilateral pressure at the laryngospasm notch (posterior to the earlobe) is a plausible but not rigorously proven maneuver; escalate to succinylcholine + intubation if ventilation fails.
  • Apnea/hypoventilation (most common with propofol, fentanyl, or fentanyl+benzodiazepine): airway repositioning, supplemental O₂, BVM support; reversal agents available but rarely needed with careful titration.
  • Ketamine emergence reactions — prophylactic midazolam is NOT recommended. Current evidence shows co-administered midazolam doesn't reduce emergence-reaction incidence and increases respiratory-complication risk when combined with ketamine — reserve benzodiazepines to treat a reaction if one occurs, not to prevent it routinely.
